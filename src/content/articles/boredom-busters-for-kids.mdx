“I’m bored.” You suggest five activities. Every one gets a no, and suddenly finding something to do has become your job.

**Give kids six familiar choices they can start with supplies they already know: build, make, read, move, solve, or pretend.** Practice choosing and getting started together, then leave the card where they can check it themselves.

<BoredomChoices />

These suggestions are for school-aged children using familiar materials. Choose what fits your child’s ability and your space; starting independently still means an adult is available when needed.

## Set up one choice before adding six

Choose a category your child already enjoys. Put its supplies within reach and show what starting looks like: get the bin, choose a spot, begin. Keep scissors, small parts, and other materials that need your help out of the independent choices.

You don’t need six new baskets. A shelf for books, a box of blocks, and paper beside the crayons may already cover half the card. The work here is teaching where things belong and what your child can use without asking.

## Six ways to get the first idea going

- **Build something:** Make a garage for three toy cars, a bridge for a stuffed animal, or a room from a cardboard box an adult has prepared.
- **Make something:** Draw a menu for an imaginary café or fold paper into a little book and fill it with pictures.
- **Read something:** Reread a favorite, look through a picture book, or choose a comic. Reading needn’t mean finishing a whole book.
- **Move your body:** Clear a safe space for ten jumps and a silly dance. Choose seated movements if those suit your child better.
- **Solve something:** Start a familiar puzzle, find objects in a hidden-picture book, or arrange blocks into a pattern to copy.
- **Play pretend:** Open a stuffed-animal shop. Make paper price tags and use pretend money you already have.

Offer one starter when needed. **Your child can decide what happens next.** Avoid turning every idea into a lesson, craft project, or finished product to inspect.

## What to say when every choice gets a no

Try: “You can pick from the card or think of your own idea. I’m available if you need help getting something down.” Give them a little room to decide. The card offers options; it doesn’t guarantee instant interest.

For a child still learning to start, offer just two choices and stay nearby for the first few minutes. Use the same gradual handoff as [teaching kids independence](/resources/teaching-kids-independence): show, practice, then step back as they’re ready.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When the card isn’t helping">

**They want company?** Ask whether they need an idea or a few minutes with you. A choice card doesn’t replace connection.

**The room gets buried?** Keep one activity out at a time and practice putting it away before choosing another.

**Low-energy afternoon?** Start with books or a familiar puzzle. There’s no need to try every category.

</WhenLifeHappens>
