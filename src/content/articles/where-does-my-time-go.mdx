It’s 9 pm and you can’t say where the day went. Nothing big happened. You answered “what time is practice?” three times, looked for the permission slip twice, and decided dinner at 5:40, again.

Here’s the short answer: **for three days, tally the small things you do twice: the questions you answer again, the things you look for, the decisions you make again. Then choose the top three to address together: put the answer where everyone can find it and agree on who handles the recurring job.**

It’s one part of [a simple system for the family’s week](/resources/how-to-organize-your-familys-week), and it works best as a reset for a command center that has stopped doing its job.

<Shift title="Track the repeats, not the hours.">

Most time audits log your day in hour blocks. That misses where it actually goes: the same question, the same search, the same decision, a few minutes at a time. **A repeat may point to hidden information or unclear responsibility.** Put the answer where the family can find it, then practice checking there. Writing it down helps; it doesn’t change the habit by itself.

</Shift>

<RealLifeVersion title="The three-day leak check">

1. **Put a card by the calendar with three columns.** Asked again, Looked for, Decided again. Under each, tally the specific repeat, not just the category.
2. **Tally, don’t time.** One mark each time something comes back, with a word to remember it by. No minutes, no app.
3. **After three days, circle the three biggest repeats.** Count the marks for each question, missing item or decision, not the total for each column.
4. **Give each one a fix, by column.** Asked again: write the answer on the calendar. Looked for: give it one home at the command center. Decided again: decide it once, in [your weekly family reset](/resources/weekly-family-reset).
5. **Reset the command center in about 30 minutes.** Clear out expired papers and flyers. Write the next few weeks’ must-keeps on the calendar: school days off, practices, who drives. Put the three fixes in place, then walk through the changes together and agree on who keeps each part current.

**Example: a completed three-day tally**

| Repeat | Marks in three days | First fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asked again: “What time is practice?” | `||||` (4) | Write time, location and driver on the shared calendar. |
| Looked for: permission slip | `|||` (3) | Give forms one “Needs attention” pocket. |
| Decided again: “What’s for dinner?” | `|||||` (5) | Choose dinners at the weekly family reset. |

These numbers are **illustrative**, not results from a real family's time audit. Your repeats may look completely different. Write one line per repeat, add a mark when it happens, then choose your three biggest.

Notice that every fix moves the answer out of your head and onto the wall. Kids who can read can check it themselves, which is what lets [someone else take over a whole job](/resources/how-to-share-the-mental-load) later. No command center yet? Start with [the smallest useful one](/resources/home-command-center).

## Turn a repeated question into a shared job

1. **Choose one repeat together.** If everyone asks about practice, agree on who keeps that activity’s dates and gear current.
2. **Show where the answer belongs.** Walk through one update and tomorrow’s check. A child can learn to find the time and prepare their own bag.
3. **Move the maintenance too.** The owner updates changes and uses their own reminder. At the weekly check-in, review whether the fix helped instead of keeping a permanent audit going.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When the check doesn’t go to plan">

**Only managed one day of marks? Fix the top one.** One visible answer gives the family somewhere to check. Practice that habit before adding more fixes.

**The question came back? Check the location and the agreement.** Is the answer easy to find? Does the person know where to look or own the follow-through? Review those before doing more setup.

**Sick week or a week off the rails? Skip the check.** Run it next week. A week that’s all exceptions won’t show you the usual leaks.

</WhenLifeHappens>
