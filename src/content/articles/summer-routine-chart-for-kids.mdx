By the second week of summer, someone is asking for a screen before breakfast, the shoes are missing, and every activity seems to need you to organize it.

**A good summer routine chart for kids is a loose sequence of six daily anchors, not a minute-by-minute schedule.** Keep the order familiar, let the times flex and give children a few jobs they can manage themselves.

<Tip label="Before you start" title="Use pictures for kids who don't read yet.">

A sun for morning, shoes for outside, a book for quiet time and a basket for tidy-up can work better than a long written list. Make the chart together so your child knows what each symbol means.

</Tip>

## A sample summer weekday routine

This is an example for a family with elementary-aged children, not a rule about how many hours anyone should spend playing or learning.

| Part of day | What kids can expect |
| --- | --- |
| Morning | Breakfast, get dressed, brush teeth |
| After breakfast | One responsibility, then independent play |
| Late morning | Outside time, errands or an outing |
| After lunch | Quiet time: books, puzzles or rest |
| Afternoon | Creative play, friends, or agreed screen time |
| Evening | Help with dinner, quick tidy-up, bedtime routine |

**Keep the order; change the clock.** Camp days, swim lessons, visits with friends and rainy days won't follow the same times. The chart still tells everyone what comes next.

<Shift title="A summer routine isn't supposed to fill every hour.">

It gives kids enough predictability to make their own next choice, and gives you fewer decisions to carry.

</Shift>

## Make it a chart they can use without asking you

Put the chart at your child's height, near breakfast or the place where they start their day. Use one row for each anchor. Skip twenty tiny tasks.

Let children choose between two options within an anchor: bike ride or playground, books or coloring. Adults still set the boundaries, but kids can begin managing the ordinary choices.

**Try this after breakfast:** Your child checks the next picture, finishes one helping job, then chooses a play activity. They don't need you to narrate the whole morning.

## What changes for different ages?

- **Ages 3–5:** Use three to five pictures with an adult nearby. Focus on breakfast, getting ready, play, rest and bedtime.
- **Ages 6–8:** Use five or six anchors. Let kids check off simple jobs and choose between two activities.
- **Ages 9–12:** Let children help shape the schedule, own a household job and prepare for outings.

Adjust for your child's development, support needs and your actual family schedule. Our [chores by age guide](/resources/chores-for-kids-by-age) offers practical job ideas.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When summer doesn't follow the chart">

**Rain cancels the park?** Keep the active block but switch to an indoor game or dance break.

**Camp starts early?** Use only the morning and evening anchors.

**Everyone slept in?** Keep the sequence and drop the clock times. You don't need to catch up.

</WhenLifeHappens>
