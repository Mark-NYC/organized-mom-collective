The dentist is in your phone. The practice change is in a text from the coach. The field trip form is still in a backpack. Nobody else knows any of it, so everyone asks you.

Here’s the short answer: **use one calendar everyone can access, write entries in a consistent way, and review the week together. Include who handles the ride, preparation or deadline.**

It’s the first piece of [a simple system for the family’s week](/resources/how-to-organize-your-familys-week).

<Shift title="A family calendar is for reading, not just writing.">

Most advice is about the format: paper or app, which colors, which categories. But a calendar only one person keeps is just a record. **The test is simple: can anyone in the house read tomorrow without asking you?**

</Shift>

<RealLifeVersion title="How to set up a family calendar">

1. **Pick one calendar.** Paper or digital both work. Agree on the family’s shared reference. You can keep a work calendar separately; be clear about which plans also belong on the family calendar.
2. **Make it easy to check.** Hang a paper calendar by the fridge or the door, with a pen within reach. For a digital calendar, make sure the adults who need it can open and update it.
3. **Write every entry the same way.** Time, what, who, and what to bring. For example:

   **3:30 · Soccer · Sam, Dad drives · cleats, water**

4. **Put on it what changes the day.** Leave off what never changes:

| On the calendar | Off the calendar |
| --- | --- |
| School runs, pick-ups, rides | Every-day bedtimes and routines |
| Practices, lessons, appointments | Long to-do lists |
| Birthdays, parties, forms due | Notes that need a paragraph |
| Who’s home late, dinner on busy nights | Anything nobody needs to act on |

5. **Colors are optional.** If you use them, one per person, and black for the whole family.
6. **Look at the week before it starts.** Start with five minutes on Sunday: who drives where, which nights are short, what needs packing. Use [the full weekly reset](/resources/weekly-family-reset) when you’re ready to plan the rest. The short nights are the ones to [plan easy dinners for](/resources/weekly-meal-planning-for-families).

Keep incoming forms beside it in [a small home command center](/resources/home-command-center). The person receiving an update adds it, or agrees on who will. Practice checking tomorrow together. Children who are learning to read can look with an adult; independent checking grows with practice.

Each day’s cleaning zone can live at the bottom of its column too, so the [weekly cleaning schedule](/resources/weekly-cleaning-schedule-for-busy-moms) is where everyone can see it.

</RealLifeVersion>

## Teach the family to check and update it

1. **Practice tomorrow’s plan together.** Have each person find their activity and what they need. Read with children who need help.
2. **Share the updating.** Agree that the person who receives a changed time adds it or confirms who will. Adults can own particular school or activity updates.
3. **Use the calendar as the first stop.** Point to the entry instead of reciting the schedule. Once the habit is familiar, each person checks their own plans and preparation.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When the calendar falls behind">

**Missed the Sunday look? Just look at tomorrow tonight.** Do the full week next weekend.

**Plans change midweek? Cross it out and write the new time beside it,** so everyone sees it changed.

**Off the calendar for weeks?** Start with this week. Don’t fill in what already happened.

</WhenLifeHappens>
