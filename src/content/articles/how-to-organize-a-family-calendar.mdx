The dentist is in your phone. The practice change is in a text from the coach. Nobody else knows any of it, so everyone asks you.

**Use one calendar everyone can access, write entries in a consistent way, and review the week together. Include who handles the ride, preparation or deadline.**

When you’re ready to plan the rest, use [the full weekly reset](/resources/weekly-family-reset); the short nights are the ones to [plan easy dinners for](/resources/weekly-meal-planning-for-families).

<Shift title="A family calendar is for reading, not just writing.">

Most advice is about the format: paper or app, which colors, which categories. But a calendar only one person keeps is just a record. **The test is simple: can anyone in the house read tomorrow without asking you?**

</Shift>

## Write every entry the same way

Time, what, who, and what to bring:

> 3:30 · Soccer · Sam, Dad drives · cleats, water

## Teach the family to check and update it

The person who receives an update adds it, or agrees on who will. **Children who are learning to read can look with an adult; independent checking grows with practice.**

1. **Practice tomorrow’s plan together.** Have each person find their activity and what they need. Read with children who need help.
2. **Share the updating.** Agree that the person who receives a changed time adds it or confirms who will. Adults can own particular school or activity updates.
3. **Use the calendar as the first stop.** Point to the entry instead of reciting the schedule. Once the habit is familiar, each person checks their own plans and preparation.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When the calendar falls behind">

**Missed the Sunday look? Just look at tomorrow tonight.** Do the full week next weekend.

**Plans change midweek? Cross it out and write the new time beside it,** so everyone sees it changed.

**Off the calendar for weeks?** Start with this week. Don’t fill in what already happened.

</WhenLifeHappens>
