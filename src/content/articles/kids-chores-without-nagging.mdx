The chart is on the fridge. Your child knows how to clear the table. Dinner ends, everyone walks away, and you’re still the one saying what needs to happen next.

**To get kids to do chores without nagging, start with one manageable job, teach what finished looks like, attach it to a familiar daily moment, and practice checking a visible reminder. Reduce your prompts as your child becomes more capable; doing a chore independently and remembering to start it are separate things to practice.**

## Why does my child only do chores when I ask?

A child may know where the plates go and still need help noticing that it’s time to move them. Or they may understand “clear your place” differently than you do. Before repeating yourself, check what’s actually missing.

Can they explain the job? Can they do it with you nearby? Do they know when it begins? Can they tell when it’s finished?

If they can do it after a reminder, focus on the starting cue. If they get stuck halfway through, focus on that step. If they argue every time, make room for a calm conversation about the expectation and what’s making it difficult.

The goal is a familiar responsibility they gradually learn to carry. That’s one small part of [building family rhythms that make room for everyone](/resources/intentional-family-life), rather than leaving the household’s remembering with one person.

## Start with one job your child can learn to own

Choose something small, useful, and repeated often enough to practice. Clearing their place after dinner is a clearer starting point than “help with the kitchen.” Putting dirty clothes in the hamper is more manageable than “keep your room clean.”

Offer a choice between two jobs that genuinely need doing: “Would you like to put out the napkins or clear your place?” Once you agree, keep the assignment steady while they learn it.

**One job is a starting experiment, not a rule about how many chores every child needs.** Their experience, ability, schedule, and support needs matter. Don’t add three unfamiliar tasks because the first one went well once.

Other adults can teach and support this routine too. Agree on the same cue and finish line so practicing it doesn’t become another job only Mom can do.

## Teach what “done” looks like

Show the task when nobody is rushing. Explain the few steps that matter, then practice together. Let your child handle the parts they can do while you help with the unfamiliar ones.

For clearing their place, your family might agree on:

1. Scrape leftover food into the agreed bin.
2. Put the plate, cup, and utensils in the safe place you’ve chosen.
3. Wipe crumbs from their spot with a damp cloth.

That’s an illustrative routine, not a requirement to copy. A younger child might simply carry an unbreakable cup to a low counter. An older child might be ready to load their dishes.

Ask, “Can you show me how you’ll know you’re finished?” A quick picture of the cleared place can help explain your agreement. Accept a child’s workable result, even if you would fold the cloth differently.

For a fuller teaching sequence, follow our guide to [showing, practicing, and handing over one responsibility](/resources/teaching-kids-independence).

## Give the chore a cue that isn’t Mom

Give each familiar job a clear place in the day. Use something your child can notice, such as getting up from dinner or taking off their shoes after school.

| Job | When it starts | What finished looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear their place | When they finish dinner | Their dishes are in the agreed spot and crumbs are wiped up. |
| Put shoes and coat away | When they come home | Shoes are on the rack; coat is on the reachable hook. |
| Put dirty clothes away | When they change for bed | Dirty clothes are inside the hamper. |
| Put toys away | Before the bedtime story | The toys they used are back in their bins. |

Start with one row. Put supplies within reach and leave enough time to do the task. A hook your child can’t reach or a packed evening with no transition time needs a practical adjustment.

Use these examples to write your own sentence: **“When ___ happens, I ___; I’m finished when ___.”** Say it together and put it where the job happens.

## How do I get my child to check their chore chart?

A chart can hold the instructions, but your child still needs to learn to use it. Practice the whole sequence: notice the cue, look at the chart, do the job, check the result, and mark it complete.

At first, you might walk over together. Later, try “Check your chart” instead of listing every step. As they become familiar with it, pause and give them an opportunity to start before you prompt. Keep helping where support is still needed.

Put the chart where your child can see and reach it. A picture reminder beside the hamper may be more useful than a long list on the kitchen fridge. Use pictures or simple words your child understands.

The [American Academy of Pediatrics recommends visual chore reminders and clear expectations](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/Chores-and-Responsibility.aspx). A chart supports the teaching; it doesn’t replace practice or guarantee independent follow-through.

## What if my child forgets, refuses, or leaves a chore unfinished?

Those situations need different responses. Start by finding out which one you’re dealing with.

### They forgot to start

Use a brief prompt toward the cue or chart. If the same job keeps getting missed, check the setup: Is the reminder visible? Does the cue happen reliably? Is the job scheduled during a scramble?

Try one adjustment before replacing the whole system. Move the picture beside the coat hook, for example, and practice the arrival routine again.

### They refuse to do it

Talk when everyone is calm. Ask what feels difficult, listen, and restate the agreed job. Offer a small choice about getting started: “Do you want to carry the cup first or the plate?”

For a familiar job, you can use an agreed order: “We clear our places, then we choose the family game.” Keep that predictable rather than inventing a bigger threat each time they resist. Basic needs and affection never depend on chore completion.

A clearer chart won’t resolve every conflict. If refusal continues, revisit the task, timing, and support your child needs instead of assuming they need a stricter chart.

### They marked it done, but it isn’t finished

Return to the finish line you agreed on: “Your plate is put away. What’s our last step?” Let your child identify and complete what’s missing.

If they can’t do the remaining step, teach it again or make the job smaller. If the result is safe and meets your agreement, leave it. Quietly redoing everything gives them little chance to learn to check their own work.

## What chores are realistic at different ages?

These are starting examples from our [chores-for-kids-by-age guide](/resources/chores-for-kids-by-age), not deadlines for independence. A child can be ready to do a task while still needing help remembering it.

| Age | A possible first job | Support to practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2–3 | Put a few toys in their bin | Do it together and show where each toy belongs. |
| 4–5 | Put dirty clothes in the hamper | Keep the hamper reachable and use a picture cue. |
| 6–8 | Set and clear the table | Practice your family’s steps and safe dish handling. |
| 9–12 | Fold and put away their laundry | Agree on where things belong and what completes the job. |

Adjust to the child in front of you. Keep sharp tools, hot surfaces, and cleaning products under appropriate adult control. If a task repeatedly overwhelms your child, shorten it or add support.

## Should I pay or reward kids for chores?

You don’t have to build a points system to start this routine. Families make different choices about allowance and extra paid jobs. Whichever approach you choose, your child still needs to understand the task, its cue, and what counts as finished.

Notice specific progress: “You checked your chart and cleared your place before I asked. The table is ready for the next person.” Avoid turning praise into a comparison with a sibling.

Our guide to [chore charts without rewards](/resources/chore-chart-without-rewards) covers everyday family contributions. If your family wants a celebration or incentive, use the [simple chore reward ideas](/resources/chore-chart-reward-ideas) without creating another system you have to administer.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When the week falls apart">

**You’re late or someone is sick.** Help with what needs doing. Pick up the familiar routine at the next suitable opportunity; missed boxes don’t create a backlog.

**They need more support than expected.** Keep the same small job and practice longer. There’s no promised number of days until chores happen independently.

**Nobody is checking the chart anymore.** Return to one useful job, one visible cue, and a clear finish line. Ask what made the plan hard to use before printing a replacement.
