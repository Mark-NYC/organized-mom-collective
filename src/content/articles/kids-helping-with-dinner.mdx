It's 5:30, dinner is getting started, and someone asks, "Can I help?" Giving them something to do can seem harder than finishing it yourself.

**Start with one safe, age-appropriate dinner job, show your child what finished looks like, practice together, and let them own that small contribution before adding another.**

<Tip label="Before you start" title="Safety comes before independence.">

Everyone washes hands before handling food. Keep raw meat separate from ready-to-eat food, and supervise children's kitchen work. Age ranges are starting points, not a guarantee that a particular child is ready. See the [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics kitchen safety guidance](https://www.eatright.org/food/home-food-safety/safe-cooking-and-prep/teaching-kids-to-cook).

</Tip>

## Pick a job that is actually useful

One child could own napkins every dinner. Another could help rinse produce on salad nights. **The point is a real contribution, not keeping kids busy while you do everything else.**

## Make one job part of dinner

Choose a regular cue, such as when someone begins cooking. Keep supplies within reach and agree what counts as finished. For a simple way to [teach the job and gradually step back](/resources/teaching-kids-independence), practice it on an unrushed evening.

<WhenLifeHappens title="When dinner is already running late">

**No time to teach tonight?** Give your child the job they already know, even if it's just carrying napkins.

**Something spills?** Clean it together and try a smaller amount next time. A mistake doesn't cancel the job.

**Nobody wants to help?** Offer two manageable choices, then agree on one for the next dinner instead of turning the meal into an argument.

</WhenLifeHappens>
