Dinner’s done, plates are still on the table, and you’re asking again: “Can someone help?” Most kids can do more than they’re handed. They need a job that fits their age, a clear finish line and somewhere to check it that isn’t your voice.

**Here’s the short answer: toddlers can put toys and clothes where they go, preschoolers can finish small jobs like clearing their plate, 6–8-year-olds can own a short routine, and 9–12-year-olds can take on real household jobs like laundry and trash.** Pick three, teach them, and put them on a chart your child checks themselves.

## Age-appropriate chores, age by age

Each age has two lists: jobs most kids can do on their own once they’ve practiced, and jobs they do with you beside them or with an adult handling one part. Every child moves at their own pace, so the younger list is always good practice.

### Chores for toddlers (ages 2–3)

At this age a chore is something you do together. It will take longer than doing it yourself. That’s the practice.

<ChoresByAge band="2-3" />

### Chores for preschoolers (ages 4–5)

Look for jobs with a clear finish: the plate is at the sink, the coat is on the hook.

<ChoresByAge band="4-5" />

### Chores for 6, 7 and 8 year olds

A 6-year-old may need you nearby for the first few weeks. An 8-year-old who has practiced can often run a whole short routine, like clearing up after dinner.

<ChoresByAge band="6-8" />

### Chores for kids 9 to 12

Older kids can do real jobs for the family, not only their own things. Hand over the whole job, including noticing when it needs doing.

<ChoresByAge band="9-12" />

Chores are one way to [make room for shared family life](/resources/intentional-family-life): everyone has a part, including the kids.

<RealLifeVersion title="How to teach responsibility without nagging">

Nagging starts when a job lives in your head and nowhere else. Put it on the chart and into a routine, and the reminder stops depending on you.

1. **Pick three jobs.** One they can already do and two to learn, from their age list. Three builds the habit without a wall of boxes.
2. **Tie each job to a moment.** “After breakfast,” “after dinner,” “before bath.” A job with a when gets done; “sometime today” gets nagged.
3. **Show what done looks like.** Do it together the first few times and agree on the finish line: the plate is by the sink, not on the counter.
4. **Let the chart do the asking.** Ask “What’s on your chart?” instead of listing the jobs. A child who can’t read yet can follow it with you.
5. **Step back as they learn.** Let them tick their own boxes. Other caregivers use the same chart and the same question, so the reminding never falls to one person.

### Example: a responsibility chart for a 7-year-old

| When | Job |
| --- | --- |
| After getting dressed | Pull up the covers and put the pillow back |
| After dinner | Put your plate and cup by the sink |
| Laundry day | Put clean clothes in their drawers |

<WhenLifeHappens title="When the chart stops working">

**Missed a day? Start with today.** Don’t make up yesterday’s boxes. A blank square is information, not a debt.

**Busy week? Keep one job.** On a late practice night, it’s just the plate to the sink. The habit survives; the rest can wait.

**Pushback? Shrink the job and practice again.** “Clean your room” becomes “books back on the shelf.” A job that keeps getting skipped is usually too big or not taught yet.

## Chores for kids: common questions

### What chores are age appropriate for my child?

Use the lists above as a starting point, then watch your child. A job fits if they can do it safely and finish it after some practice. If they can’t yet, do it together or try the younger list.

### How many chores should a child have?

Three daily jobs is a good start for most kids, plus one weekly job once they’re older. Add a new one when the current jobs happen without reminders. A short list done every day teaches more than a long one done sometimes.

### Should kids get rewards or an allowance for chores?

That’s a family decision, and either can work. Some families keep everyday jobs unpaid, as part of living together, and pay for extra jobs. If you use rewards, keep them small and tied to the week, like choosing Friday’s dinner. The free chart has a celebration box for that.

### How do I motivate my child to do chores?

Make the job theirs: their name on the chart, their tick in the box. Notice the effort out loud (“You cleared your plate without being asked”) and celebrate the week together. A child who knows exactly what to do, and sees that it matters to the family, has less to push back on.

### What chores can a 6-year-old do?

Once they’ve practiced, many 6-year-olds can make their bed, set and clear the table, feed a pet and pack their backpack. With you nearby, they can unload the dishwasher, wipe the bathroom sink and vacuum their room.

### What chores can an 8-year-old do?

An 8-year-old can usually do most of the ages 6–8 list on their own and start jobs from the 9–12 list with you, like folding laundry, taking out the trash or loading the dishwasher.
